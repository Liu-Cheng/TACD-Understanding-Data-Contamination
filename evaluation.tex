We compare the CatBoost ensemble with the ten individual detectors described in Section~\ref{sec:methods} on the golden test sets. The ensemble is trained on the combined training splits of CodeV and OriGen, using the data partitioning and hyperparameters specified in Sections~\ref{sec:golden_dataset} and~\ref{sec:catboost}. The RTLCoder data are excluded from ensemble training.

We report the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) and the F1 score, treating contaminated samples as the positive class. AUC-ROC evaluates the ranking of continuous detector scores without fixing a decision threshold, whereas F1 evaluates binary predictions at the selected threshold. Higher values indicate better detection performance for both metrics.

For the ensemble, the decision threshold \(\tau^*\) is selected by five-fold stratified cross-validation on the combined CodeV and OriGen training splits. In each fold, the ensemble is fitted on four folds and produces contamination probabilities for the held-out fold. We select the threshold that maximizes the mean validation F1 across the five folds and use the decision rule \(\hat{y}=\mathbf{1}[p(x)\geq\tau^*]\). The selected threshold is fixed when evaluating the test sets and estimating benchmark contamination rates; test-set labels are not used for threshold selection.


\subsection{Detection Effectiveness and Cross-Model Generalization}
\label{sec:performance}

We evaluate the ensemble in two settings: RTL-generation variants derived from the shared foundation model DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5, and additional RTL-generation models based on CodeLlama, CodeQwen, and Qwen. Together, these experiments assess the improvement in contamination detection and the transferability of the learned detector across target models.

\subsubsection{Detection on DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5 Variants}

Table~\ref{tab:compare_baseline} compares the ten individual detectors with our CatBoost ensemble on CodeV, RTLCoder, and OriGen, all derived from DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5. The ensemble is trained on the training splits of CodeV and OriGen and evaluated on the separate golden test sets of all three models. RTLCoder contributes no samples to ensemble training, allowing us to assess transfer to another RTL-generation variant of the same foundation model.

The individual detectors exhibit different strengths in ranking and binary classification. DC-PDD achieves the highest average baseline AUC (0.850), whereas Min-K\% achieves the highest average baseline F1 (0.760). Perplexity also yields a competitive average F1 of 0.754. These results show that the detector with the strongest ranking performance does not necessarily provide the most accurate binary predictions at its operating threshold, motivating the combination of multiple detection signals.

Our ensemble outperforms every individual detector in both metrics on all three test sets. It achieves an average AUC of 0.927 and an average F1 of 0.872, improving over the strongest baseline for each metric by 0.077 and 0.112, respectively. On RTLCoder, the ensemble achieves an AUC of 0.912 and an F1 of 0.859 despite being trained only on CodeV and OriGen data. These results demonstrate that combining complementary signals improves contamination detection and supports transfer across RTL-generation variants of a shared foundation model.

\subsubsection{Generalization across RTL-Generation Models}

We further apply the trained ensemble to three additional RTL-generation models: HAVEN\_CodeLlama, HAVEN\_CodeQwen, and VeriCoder, based on CodeLlama, CodeQwen, and Qwen, respectively. None of these models contributes data to ensemble training. This evaluation tests whether the detection signals learned from CodeV and OriGen remain useful beyond DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5 variants.

Table~\ref{tab:HAVEN_VeriCoder} reports the results. The ensemble achieves AUC scores of 0.877, 0.876, and 0.888, and F1 scores of 0.796, 0.785, and 0.795, respectively, yielding an average AUC of 0.880 and an average F1 of 0.792. It achieves the highest AUC on each of the three models among the reported methods and the highest average F1, although some individual detectors achieve higher F1 on VeriCoder. Thus, the benefit of combining signals is evident across the evaluated models without requiring the ensemble to dominate every metric on every target.

Together, the two evaluations demonstrate improved detection effectiveness and strong cross-model generalization. The results suggest that the ensemble captures contamination signals that transfer across RTL-generation models built on different foundation models, rather than being tied to DeepSeek-Coder-v1.5 variants. This supports its use for auditing additional target models without retraining the ensemble for each model.

\subsection{Data Contamination in RTL Code-Generation Model Evaluation}
\label{sec:analysis}

We now use the validated ensemble to address the central empirical question of this work: how much potential training-data exposure affects the evaluation of current RTL code-generation models? Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_detection} reports estimated contamination rates for nine models on six benchmark variants. Each rate is the fraction of benchmark samples flagged for a particular target model. The overall summary is 52.0\%, obtained by averaging the models' sample-weighted rates across benchmarks. This aggregate indicates a substantial evaluation risk, but the variation across model--benchmark pairs is more informative about its possible origins and consequences.

\textbf{Training-data exposure can arise at multiple stages.}
High estimates are not confined to RTL-specialized models: DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B and Qwen2.5-Coder reach weighted averages of 74.5\% and 58.2\%, respectively. In contrast, CodeGen-6B-Multi and Phi-2 yield only 0.2\% and 6.2\%. This contrast is consistent with differences in the coverage of public RTL sources by general code-training corpora, suggesting that benchmark exposure may already be inherited from a foundation model before RTL-specific fine-tuning. Among specialized models, RTLCoder and VeriGen reach 84.4\% and 79.1\%, whereas ChipGPT and OriGen yield 41.5\% and 56.7\%. Thus, specialization alone does not explain the ordering. Domain-specific training may introduce additional overlapping examples or reinforce memorization, but its effect depends on the underlying model, data sources, and filtering practices. These comparisons motivate auditing both pretraining exposure and fine-tuning data; they do not isolate the causal contribution of either stage without matched checkpoints and training records.

\textbf{Shared data sources provide a plausible explanation for persistent exposure.}
VerilogEval draws its problems from HDLBits, a public collection of digital-design exercises~\cite{liu2023verilogeval}. Such publicly accessible tasks can overlap with material collected for model training even if the benchmark itself is excluded, because a task's source implementation may predate its benchmark release. This mechanism is consistent with the high estimates for several models on both VerilogEval versions. RTLCoder, for example, reaches 97.4\% and 93.0\%, while VeriGen reaches 94.2\% and 91.0\%. The persistence across versions also shows why updating a benchmark does not by itself establish independence from training data. VerilogEval v2 extends the original problems to specification-to-RTL tasks~\cite{pinckney2024revisitingverilogeval}; changes to prompting can change detector scores without removing prior exposure to the underlying design. Moreover, the mean estimate falls from 65.4\% to 53.5\% between VerilogEval versions but rises from 51.7\% to 54.4\% between RTLLM versions. These opposing trends argue against interpreting a newer benchmark version as automatically less exposed.

\textbf{Real-world designs offer a different evaluation regime, with lower estimated exposure.}
The averages for RealBench module- and system-level tasks are 14.4\% and 2.8\%, respectively. The contrast also holds within models: RTLCoder drops from 97.4\% on VerilogEval v1 to 30.0\% on RealBench modules, while DeepSeek-Coder-6.7B drops from 89.7\% to 18.3\%. This pattern suggests that the high estimates are associated with particular combinations of training exposure and evaluation content, rather than simply with models that receive high detector scores on every input. RealBench uses complex open-source IP designs and manually prepared, detailed specifications~\cite{jin2025realbench}. A plausible explanation is that such design-specific contexts and implementation combinations are less frequently reproduced in training corpora than individual public exercises. Its later release may also reduce exposure to the benchmark's specification--implementation pairs for models trained earlier, although the underlying open-source IP can still have been encountered. These factors help explain why practical design tasks may probe capabilities that are less well represented by familiar public exercises. However, longer and more complex inputs can also alter detector behavior, and one of only four system-level samples accounts for VeriGen's 25.0\% estimate. The low system-level average therefore warrants further validation on more designs.

\textbf{Contamination can confound model comparisons and apparent progress.}
On VerilogEval v1, the estimates for RTLCoder and ChipGPT differ by 39.7 percentage points (97.4\% versus 57.7\%). Consequently, even a shared test set may mix prior exposure and generalization in different proportions for different models. A high aggregate pass rate could reflect strong performance on familiar designs while providing limited evidence about performance on new RTL tasks. The risk is particularly acute for model--benchmark pairs with near-saturated contamination estimates, where few samples remain unflagged. Conversely, a low estimated rate does not imply better generation ability. Contamination rates alone cannot quantify score inflation or determine whether a model ranking would change after controlling for exposure; this requires linking sample-level detection results to functional correctness.

These findings motivate reporting contamination alongside generation performance for each model--benchmark pair. A useful next evaluation is to separate flagged and unflagged samples while accounting for task difficulty and the number of remaining samples. Since each model may have encountered different examples, filtering each model's test set independently can itself distort comparisons; a common audited subset or newly sourced tasks would provide a stronger basis for comparing generalization. The practical implication is that progress in RTL generation should be assessed jointly with training-data exposure, rather than inferred from benchmark pass rates alone.

To understand the contribution of each design choice, we conduct three ablation experiments: (1) choice of boosting algorithm, (2) method subset selection, and (3) training source and data size.

\subsubsection{Choice of Boosting Algorithm}
\label{sec:choice}

Table~\ref{tab:boosting_algorithms} compares the performance of CatBoost, XGBoost, and LightGBM. CatBoost achieves the highest average AUC at 0.927, marginally ahead of XGBoost at 0.924, and delivers the highest average F1 at 0.872, showing a more substantial advantage over XGBoost at 0.859. LightGBM trails on both metrics, with an average AUC of 0.914 and an average F1 of 0.857. While XGBoost yields a slightly higher AUC on test set of CodeV , CatBoost demonstrates superior performance on the two remaining test sets and, more importantly, achieves substantially higher F1 scores across all three test sets, indicating better binary classification performance at the deployed threshold. Notably, on RTLCoder, the unseen black-box target model i.e. CatBoost outperforms XGBoost in both AUC and F1, a gap we attribute to CatBoost's ordered target encoding, which eliminates prediction shift and yields more calibrated probability estimates on out-of-distribution data. In contrast, XGBoost's standard target encoding tends to overfit source-domain patterns, resulting in relatively weaker generalization to unseen targets. LightGBM consistently underperforms relative to both CatBoost and XGBoost across the majority of metrics and test sets. Based on these results, we adopt CatBoost as our default ensemble algorithm accordingly.

We begin with DC-PDD, which achieves the highest average AUC among all individual baselines in Section~\ref{sec:comparison}, and iteratively add the method that yields the largest increase in average AUC across the three test sets. Figure~\ref{fig:greedy} visualizes the trajectory of this selection process. The average AUC improves steadily from 0.846 with DC-PDD alone, reaching a peak of 0.927 when Zlib is added as the fifth method. This optimal combination comprises DC-PDD, Reference, Neighborhood, Min-K\%, and Zlib. Beyond this point, further additions yield diminishing or even negative returns: adding Perplexity, PETAL, and CDD gradually lowers the average AUC, and while including Min-K\%++ temporarily raises the average to 0.930, this gain is most pronounced on the CodeV (0.949) and OriGen (0.970) test sets, whereas RTLCoder collapses sharply from 0.901 to 0.871. This divergence, where improvements on source-related models come at the cost of severe degradation on the unseen target, indicates that these additional methods encode signals tightly coupled with the source model architecture and introduce distributional noise that actively harms generalization to black-box target models. This five-method combination therefore strikes the optimal balance between discriminative power and cross-model stability, and is adopted as our optimal feature set.

\subsubsection{The Composition and Size of the Training Set }
\label{sec:source}



We investigate how the composition and size of the training set affect detection performance. Figure~\ref{fig:train_size} reports the AUC on each test set as a function of training data size defined as the number of samples provided by each training set per class, denoted as \(tc\) across seven training set configurations.

For most training set configurations, the average AUC exhibits a general upward trend as \(tc\) increases from 50 to 400, with the most substantial gains occurring in the low-data regime (\(tc \leq 200\)). However, the trend is not monotonic across all configurations: for the OriGen-only training set and the CodeV+RTLCoder training set, the average AUC declines slightly when \(tc\) increases from 350 to 400. On individual test sets, performance fluctuates across data sizes, indicating that larger training data does not guarantee improvement on every target model. Notably, the CodeV+OriGen training set achieves the highest average AUC of 0.927 at \(tc=400\). With only 100 samples per class, the CodeV+OriGen training set already achieves an average AUC of 0.900, surpassing all single-source training sets at their full data volume and underscoring the data efficiency of cross-model training.

Among all configurations, the CodeV+OriGen training set achieves the best average performance across most data sizes, outperforming all single-source and dual-source alternatives, while performing on par with the three-source configuration (CodeV+RTLCoder+OriGen). Critically, the CodeV+OriGen training set also demonstrates strong generalization to the unseen black-box target model RTLCoder, achieving an AUC of 0.912 at \(tc=400\), confirming its effectiveness in the cross-model detection scenario. Notably, adding a third training set (CodeV+RTLCoder+OriGen) yields almost identical average AUC compared to CodeV+OriGen, indicating that increasing the number of training sets does not necessarily enhance performance. Given its superior performance and validated cross-model detection effectiveness, we adopt CodeV+OriGen as the optimal training set composition.

\subsection{Ablation Analysis}
\label{sec:ablation}



\subsubsection{Feature Combination}

We sweep 22 feature subsets spanning 3 to 10 probes. Full results are reported in Table~\ref{tab:feature_sweep_full}. 



The full sweep reveals four patterns. \textbf{(1)} smaller subsets dominate: configurations with 3--6 probes rank among the best, while all 9--10 probe configurations fall below average. The 10f ensemble (Avg 0.565) is outperformed by the 3f subset (0.611), demonstrating that noisy probes actively degrade generalization. \textbf{(2)} adding Min-20\%++ causes the sharpest drop: $\mathcal{G}_5 + \text{neighbor}$ (0.667) drops to 0.546 when mink++\_0.2 is added, confirming this probe as the most harmful for cross-model transfer. \textbf{(3)} removing zlib helps VeriForge-related directions (C$\to$Vf: 0.271 $\to$ 0.474) but hurts RTLCoder-related ones, revealing a model-dependent trade-off. \textbf{(4)} Top-5 (CodeV) achieves competitive performance (Avg 0.638) despite no cross-model optimization, suggesting that source-domain probe ranking provides a useful prior.

We vary the per-class training sample count $T \in \{100, 200, 300, 400\}$ under the best configuration for both single-source and dual-source modes. Table~\ref{tab:tc_ablation} reports complete per-direction results.
